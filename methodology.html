Methodology

How this site works

We collect reports from official agencies. We make two kinds of forecast: a few made days in advance by our own weather models, and many that restate an alert an agency has already issued. Later, we check each forecast against what really happened, each by its own rule. We never claim to be certain. You can see how our forecasts turned out on the Track record page.

1 · We collect reports

We read current events and warnings from public sources. These include the GDACS, the USGS, the EMSC, NASA EONET, ReliefWeb and US weather and hurricane services. Each source covers only some hazards and places. If no source reports on a place, we cannot see what is happening there. A place with no reports is not necessarily safe.

2 · We tidy them

We give every event the same basic details: the hazard, the time, the place and the source. When two agencies report the same event, we show it once.

3 · We sort them

You can sort events by date, risk level, place or type. When you check a place, we show which events are close enough to reach it.

4 · We make forecasts

Our weather models forecast unusually hot days, unusually heavy rain and a high fire-weather index up to 7 days ahead, at 108 cities, before anything has happened (see Weather models below). Every other forecast starts from an official report: if a hazard such as a flood, storm or fire is still going on, we keep the agency’s alert open for as long as that hazard usually lasts, and say so. After an earthquake of magnitude 5.5 or more, we forecast its aftershocks for 7 days. Every forecast names the hazard, the place and the period it covers.

5 · We lock them

We save each forecast in a public record when we first make it. We only ever add to that record. So the place, the dates and the test for success cannot be changed once the outcome is known.

6 · We check them

When the forecast period ends, we check it. A weather model’s forecast is checked against the weather NASA POWER measured, because a weather line is not a disaster. Every other forecast is checked against the disasters that agencies reported. We mark it correct, partly correct or wrong only after looking at that evidence.

A forecast that restates an agency's alert counts as correct only if new damage or impact is reported inside its period. The agency still listing the hazard is the alert we restated, so it does not count. If our sources could have shown a new impact and show none, the forecast is wrong. These forecasts are not a test of our skill, and we report them apart from forecasts made in advance. Checker version verify-1.4.0.

How we mark a forecast

ResultWhat it means
CorrectThe hazard happened in the place and period the forecast named. For a weather model’s forecast: the line was crossed on at least one day of the window.
Partly correctThe hazard happened in that period, but only in part of the place.
WrongAgencies reported no such hazard in that place and period. For a weather model’s forecast (a false alarm): the line was not crossed on any day.
MissedA disaster happened that none of our forecasts named. For the weather models: a day the line was crossed at a place a model run was watching, with no forecast for it.
Cannot be checkedNo source we read covers that hazard in that place well enough to say.
Test caseThe forecast was written after the event had started, so it does not count as made in advance.

Each hazard has a fixed distance limit, for example 250 km for an earthquake and 300 km for a flood. The same limit applies to every forecast. We do not change it to fit a result. A weather model’s forecast has no distance limit: it is checked at one grid cell.

Each forecast is counted once, by the first version we recorded. A forecast we updated is recorded again, and the later versions are kept but never counted as more forecasts.

What we do not claim

No probabilities

You will not see a figure like “92% chance” here: we have no tested way to turn a forecast into a probability. Where we show a percentage, it is a measured rate from past tests, such as how often the days a model flagged did cross its line, with what it was measured on.

Risk levels instead

Every badge, filter, calendar bar and map marker uses one scale of six levels: Low, Watch, Moderate, High, Severe and Extreme. A record with no level says “Not rated”.

  • From an agency: its colour lands on the matching level (green Low, yellow Watch, orange Moderate, red High). Where that colour is the agency’s estimate of impact, the badge says whose: “Low expected impact (USGS PAGER green)”.
  • From what was reported: deaths, people displaced or affected raise the level to Moderate, Severe or Extreme, never lower it.
  • Storms: a named storm is never below its agency category. A tropical storm is at least Watch, a typhoon, hurricane or category 1 to 2 at least Moderate, category 3 at least Severe, and category 4 to 5 or a super typhoon Extreme.
  • Restated agency alerts: one step below the alert they restate, because they add nothing to it.
  • Our weather models: Moderate when a place’s 95th percentile is forecast to be crossed, High when its 99th is (for rain, when the soil is already wetter than 4 years in 5; for fire, an index of 38 or more).
  • An earthquake of magnitude 6 or more with no impact assessment yet is “Not rated”: its magnitude is a measurement, not a level.

Earthquakes cannot be predicted

No one can predict exactly when and where an earthquake will strike. We report earthquakes after they happen, and we forecast only the aftershocks that follow a strong one.

Weather models

Our only forecasts made before anything was known. They forecast weather lines, not disasters: a hit means a day crossed a local weather line, not that anyone was harmed. The code is forecast-models.js and tools/run-models.js.

InputsEvery day, the Open-Meteo forecast for the next 9 days (daily maximum temperature, rain, mean dew point and mean wind), and NASA POWER’s measurements of the last 60 days, for 108 cities. Each city’s normal is 30 years of NASA POWER records, 1996 to 2025, at the grid cell (about 50 to 70 km across) that contains it.
Bias correctionThe forecast service and NASA POWER do not agree exactly. Over the last 21 days both cover (at least 15 are needed), the average difference in temperature and dew point is removed from the forecast, and rain and wind are scaled by the ratio of the two (between 0.5 and 2). Where the temperature difference is more than 3 °C, the city is skipped that day rather than corrected.
Warming adjustmentThe heat line is moved by each city’s own warming trend over 1996 to 2025 (the median slope of its yearly mean temperature, Theil-Sen), to the year it is used in, by at most 2.5 °C. Without it the 30-year line was crossed on 14% of days rather than about 5%.
Unusually hot daysThe corrected daily high reaches the city’s 95th percentile for the date (a level it reaches about 1 day in 20) on 2 or more days in a row. High if any day reaches the 99th.
Unusually heavy rainRain in a day at or above the city’s 99th percentile for the month and at least 10 mm, or a 3-day total at or above its 99th percentile and at least 20 mm. High if the topsoil was already wetter than 80% of the same month.
High fire-weather indexThe Canadian Fire Weather Index, built from the last 60 days of measured weather and the corrected forecast, at or above 21.3 (“very high”) and the city’s 95th percentile for the month, on 2 or more days in a row. High at 38 or more. Fire also needs fuel and a spark, which no weather index can see.
Days forecastDays 1 to 7 after the run. Each forecast is recorded in our public ledger when it is published, before its first day, and never changed.
How each is gradedAgainst NASA POWER for the same grid cell, by the same rule as the forecast: correct if the line was crossed on at least one day of the window, a false alarm if not. NASA POWER revises its recent days, so a day is used only once its measurement has settled: fetched at least 14 days after the day. Until then the forecast waits. Grades and misses are both recomputed from the settled measurements on every run (tools/rebuild-model-grades.js rebuilds them from the published files).
Misses and chanceA miss is a day the line was crossed at a city a run was watching (1 to 7 days ahead, and checked that day), with no forecast for it. Every result is also given day by day, as the backtest is scored, and re-issues of one forecast are merged into one claim. Beside each forecast is what chance would give: a window of N days catches a crossing with probability 1 - (1 - p)^N, where p is the share of watched days the line was crossed.
BacktestThe method was tested on forecasts Open-Meteo really issued 3 and 5 days ahead, in 108 cities from 17 Jul to 17 Oct 2026, scored day by day against NASA POWER. One season only. Fire was tested with only the last day forecast, an easier test than the live one. Days 1, 2, 4, 6 and 7 ahead were not tested. The results are on the Track record page.
