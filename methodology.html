Methodology

How this site works

We collect reports from official agencies. We use some of them to make our own short forecasts. Later, we check each forecast against what really happened. We never claim to be certain. You can see how our forecasts turned out on the Track record page.

1 · We collect reports

We read current events and warnings from public sources. These include the GDACS, the USGS, the EMSC, NASA EONET, ReliefWeb and US weather and hurricane services. Each source covers only some hazards and places. If no source reports on a place, we cannot see what is happening there. A place with no reports is not necessarily safe.

2 · We tidy them

We give every event the same basic details: the hazard, the time, the place and the source. When two agencies report the same event, we show it once.

3 · We sort them

You can sort events by date, risk level, place or type. When you check a place, we show which events are close enough to reach it.

4 · We make forecasts

Each forecast starts from an official report. If a hazard such as a flood, storm or fire is still going on, we forecast whether it may carry on. The period depends on the hazard. It is usually a few days, and longer for a drought. After an earthquake of magnitude 5.5 or more, we forecast its aftershocks for 7 days. Every forecast names the hazard, the place and the period it covers.

5 · We lock them

We save each forecast in a public record when we first make it. We only ever add to that record. So the place, the dates and the test for success cannot be changed once the outcome is known.

6 · We check them

When the forecast period ends, we compare the forecast with the disasters that agencies reported. We mark it correct, partly correct or wrong only after looking at that evidence.

A forecast that restates an agency's alert counts as correct only if new damage or impact is reported inside its period. The agency still listing the hazard is the alert we restated, so it does not count. If our sources could have shown a new impact and show none, the forecast is wrong. These forecasts are not a test of our skill, and we report them apart from forecasts made in advance. Checker version verify-1.4.0.

How we mark a forecast

ResultWhat it means
CorrectThe hazard happened in the place and period the forecast named.
Partly correctThe hazard happened in that period, but only in part of the place.
WrongAgencies reported no such hazard in that place and period.
MissedA disaster happened that none of our forecasts named.
Cannot be checkedNo source we read covers that hazard in that place well enough to say.
Test caseThe forecast was written after the event had started, so it does not count as made in advance.

Each hazard has a fixed distance limit, for example 250 km for an earthquake and 300 km for a flood. The same limit applies to every forecast. We do not change it to fit a result.

What we do not claim

No made-up percentages

You will not see a figure like “92% chance” here. We have no tested way to turn our forecasts into percentages, so we do not show any.

Risk levels instead

We show a risk level: Low, Watch, Moderate, High, Severe or Extreme. It comes from the agency’s own alert level or from what was measured, such as wind speed. If we have no level, we say “Not rated”.

Earthquakes cannot be predicted

No one can predict exactly when and where an earthquake will strike. We report earthquakes after they happen, and we forecast only the aftershocks that follow a strong one.
